We propose \approach, a training-free \ac{LLM} workflow that recovers trace links between an architecture document and an architectural model.
Its input is a document $D$ with sentences $S = \{s_1, \ldots, s_n\}$ and a component set $C = \{c_1, \ldots, c_m\}$.
Its output is a link set $L \subseteq S \times C$.
Each link $(s_i, c_j) \in L$ asserts that sentence $s_i$ discusses the architectural responsibilities of component $c_j$.

In \autoref{fig:example}, one sentence reads ``Only the gui knows the user and his preferences.''
The sentence discusses the \texttt{gui} component, and it also contains \texttt{preferences}, the name of a component it does not discuss.
Other sentences discuss a component without naming it at all, referring back to it with a pronoun or with a single word of its name.
Documentation explains a system rather than identifying its parts, so linking each sentence to the components it names invents links and misses links at the same time.

The names are still where the evidence is, and how much of a name a sentence gives is something a program can determine exactly.
A sentence may state a component's name or an alias outright;
it may carry only one word of that name, with the name itself stated elsewhere;
or it may name nothing and refer back to an earlier sentence.
What the sentence gives fixes what evidence there is for a link, and how far that evidence can be trusted on its own.
\approach therefore splits the linking work by that form: one linker per form proposes the sentences its form reaches, and one judge per form reads the sentence and decides, no stricter than its evidence allows.

\textbf{\approach in a nutshell.}
\approach answers the three challenges in three steps (\autoref{fig:approach-overview}).
It first builds a runtime \emph{knowledge module}, an alias table computed once from the components and the document.
A sentence then gives a component's whole name, part of it, or only a reference to it, and \approach runs three linkers in that order: the \linkerB, the \linkerD, and the \linkerC, each reading the same alias table and each looking for the one evidence form it is built on.
The three link sets are then merged by pair, so a sentence two linkers reach still yields one link.
Each proposed link is checked by an \evidenceBacked judge, and the surviving links are merged into the output.

\subsection{Design of \approach}\label{sec:arch}

Three decisions fix the shape of the workflow.

The first decision answers the project-knowledge challenge: the workflow gathers what it needs to know about a project once, up front, rather than leaving each linker to work it out on its own.
The underlying difficulty is the vocabulary problem~\cite{furnas1987vocabulary}: one component can appear under many names, and some of those names are ordinary English, so plain lexical matching is unreliable.
\approach builds this knowledge before linking, as a separate stage, in the spirit of glossary mining for requirements~\cite{arora2017automated,gemkow2018automatic} and software-side synonym networks~\cite{howard2013automatically,falleri2010automatic}.
The knowledge module holds one table, the alias table. 

The second decision answers the reference-form challenge: how to split the linking work.
Let $N(c)$ be the \emph{names} of a component $c$: its model name $\mathrm{name}(c)$ and the project-specific aliases $A(c)$ that the knowledge module discovers (its abbreviations and synonyms).

A sentence may write a name in more ways than one, and \approach needs a single, stated
notion of when it has.
We write $s \models_{f} n$ when sentence $s$ \emph{writes} name $n$ at \emph{form} $f$, over two independent dimensions.
\emph{Fidelity} says how exactly the characters must reproduce the name: \emph{as spelled} (character for character, at word boundaries), \emph{any case} (ignoring capitalization), or \emph{any spelling} (the name's word sequence, ignoring case, separators and compound joining, so that \texttt{Image Provider}, \texttt{image-provider} and \texttt{ImageProvider} are one form).
\emph{Extent} says how much of the name must be present: the whole name, or \emph{any word} of it under an English inflectional ending.
This relation reads only the model catalog and the alias table, together with one closed list of English suffixes; it contains no project vocabulary of any kind.

\autoref{tab:forms} is why each linker scans the form it does.
Each row reports how many sentence--component pairs the form reaches across the five benchmark documents and how many of them are true links.
Precision falls as the form loosens and reach rises, and \approach answers that with the judge behind it rather than with a further rule: a whole name at \emph{any case} feeds a judge that approves by default, \emph{any word} feeds a judge that is shown no target, and sentences no form reaches at all are left to a judge that rejects when uncertain.
The workflow scans those two forms and no others, and nothing in this deterministic layer admits a link: it can only end a case, which it does in two places.
One is the alias table, where a term two components both claim is taken to name neither and is dropped before any linker reads it; the other is the \emph{any word} scan, which refuses a word the sentence writes only inside another component's whole name (\autoref{sec:partial-linker}).
Nothing in the relation admits a link on its own: every form produces a \emph{candidate} for an \ac{LLM} judge.

The forms are almost, but not quite, nested, and the exceptions are informative rather than incidental: compound splitting reaches \texttt{Image Provider} for a component named \texttt{ImageProvider}, which no case-folding rule reaches, and misses \texttt{redis pubsub} for one named \texttt{Redis PubSub}, which case folding does reach.
Compound splitting is therefore a different normalization, not a looser one.
\approach scans one form per linker and does not scan this one, so a component the document only ever writes split is out of \linkerB's reach and has to be recovered through the alias table.

A sentence $s$ then writes a full name in $N(c)$, part of one, or none of one.
These ask different questions (whether a sentence writes a name, whether it carries one word of a name written elsewhere, or whether it writes no name and must be resolved through the earlier sentence it refers back to), so they need different evidence and different judges.
\approach therefore uses three linkers, one per form, rather than the one large \ac{LLM} call that previous works used.
Each runs once over the whole document, and no linker is shown what the earlier ones produced: the only ordering the workflow enforces lives inside the relation, where \linkerD skips any sentence that states a whole name because that sentence is \linkerB's to judge. That is a property of the sentence, not of the link set.
Separating the forms is what lets the judges differ, since a judge can be no stricter than the evidence its own linker reports.

The third decision answers the reliability challenge: how to handle the non-determinism of the \ac{LLM}.
An \ac{LLM} linker can return a wrong link that still reads as plausible, with nothing in the sentence to support it.
\approach therefore makes each linker report the text evidence it used in a structured \emph{evidence bundle} and 
has each judge check the candidate link against that. 
Every judge must quote the sentence words it rules on before it rules, and where the sentence carries no name for the judge to fall back on, that quote is checked mechanically against the sentence (\autoref{sec:validators}).

\subsection{Project Knowledge Discovery}\label{sec:knowledge}
Stage~1 is a \emph{document-understanding module} that analyzes the document against the component set to find the aliases the project has coined for its components.

\approach leaves ordinary-English ambiguity to the judges, which see the sentence itself.

\subsubsection{Alias Discovery}\label{sec:doc-understanding}
An alias lets a linker match a document phrase to a component even when the phrase is not the component's exact name.
\approach discovers these aliases from the document at runtime, and they come in two kinds.
The first is an explicit renaming, where the document itself ties a short form to a name: an abbreviation in a parenthetical definition, or a synonym in a clear restatement.
The second is a shorthand, one word of a multi-word name used on its own after the document has introduced the name.
Shorthands are common, but many are also generic English whose meaning depends on the surrounding text, so this is the hard kind.
An \ac{LLM} judge confirms each proposed mapping before it enters the alias table, rejecting a phrase that is generic vocabulary, names the whole system, names a different entity, or names a group of elements rather than one.
Every confirmed alias is then a name of its component wherever it appears, on the same footing as the model name.

\begin{promptL}{Alias Extraction (Document Understanding)}{alias}\\
{\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
Find alternative names used for the components.\\
COMPONENTS: \{comp\_names\}\\
\{EXTRACTION\_RULE\}\\
DOCUMENT: \{doc\_lines\}\\
Return JSON:\\
~~\{"aliases": [\{term, component\}]\}
}
\end{promptL}

\subsection{\linkerB}\label{sec:entity-linker}

\linkerB reaches the sentences that state a name of a component outright.
It proposes the candidate $(s,c)$ whenever $s$ writes a name in $N(c)$ at \emph{any case}, so the proposer is a scan of the model catalog and the alias table and spends no \ac{LLM} call.
Scanning at \emph{any case} rather than at \emph{as spelled} buys reach at the cost of precision, and \approach leaves that precision to the judge, because a component named \texttt{Common} or \texttt{Client} needs the sentence read before the mention can be ruled on at all.
Each candidate is tagged with the matched sentence and the reference form, forming the candidate's evidence bundle.
Candidates and their evidence bundles will be the input for the link judge.
For example, in \autoref{fig:example} Sentence~11 names the \texttt{preferences} component, so \linkerB returns the candidate $(s_{11}, \texttt{preferences})$ with an evidence bundle recording the matched name ``preferences'' and the reference form as the reason.
Sentence~7 uses the same word ``preferences'' while its topic is \texttt{gui}.
The scan proposes $(s_{7}, \texttt{preferences})$ as well, because the sentence does write the name; deciding that the sentence is about \texttt{gui} and not about \texttt{preferences} is the judge's work, not the proposer's.

\subsection{\linkerD}\label{sec:partial-linker}

\linkerD reaches the sentences that carry only one word of a component's name.
It scans the same writing relation at its loosest form, \emph{any word}: it proposes a candidate $(s,c)$ when $s$ writes a name in $N(c)$ at that form.
It considers only sentences that write no whole name in $N(c)$, because those sentences already belong to \linkerB.
For example, a document that names an \texttt{HTML5 Client} may later write only ``each client'': \linkerD proposes that link and records the matched word as its evidence.
The scan makes one refusal of its own, on the ground the skip above already states: when every writing of the matched word falls inside a span where the sentence writes \emph{another} component's whole name, the sentence is naming that other component there, and the pair is that component's.
A sentence reading ``events are published on the \texttt{Redis PubSub} channel'' therefore yields no candidate for \texttt{Redis DB}, the sibling component whose name shares the word \texttt{Redis}; a sentence that writes the word outside the longer name as well keeps its candidate, which is why ``each client connects to the \texttt{HTML5 Server}'' still reaches \texttt{HTML5 Client}.
The refusal reads the model catalog and not the alias table, and the asymmetry is deliberate: a scan only ever proposes, so it may rest on a sampled \ac{LLM} output, whereas this predicate is the one thing in the layer that \emph{ends} a case and so may rest only on what is given.
The judge behind the scan cannot be asked instead, because it is shown no component: it calls a real participant a participant and is right, with no way to know that the participant is a different one.
One word is weak evidence on its own, since an ordinary English word may carry no architectural reference at all, so \linkerD judges the expression before it links it.
It asks, \emph{without showing the component}, whether the expression denotes a software participant or something merely associated with software.
Withholding the component is what makes the step informative: shown the target, the \ac{LLM} tends to confirm identity rather than test it (\autoref{sec:validators}).

\begin{promptL}{\linkerD: Denotation Without the Target}{denote}\\
{\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
Classify what each expression itself denotes\\
in its local context: participant for a\\
software participant, or associated for\\
something merely associated with software.\\
SENTENCES: \{Sn: text\}\\
CASES: \{case, source, expression\}\\
Claim must be an exact substring of the\\
source sentence.\\
Return JSON:\\
~~\{"judgments": [\{case, denotation,\\
~~~~claim\}]\}
}
\end{promptL}

\subsection{\linkerC}\label{sec:coref-linker}

\linkerC recovers the links in sentences that write no name at all, at any form of the relation.
For each sentence with a pronoun like 'it',  an \ac{LLM} step resolves the pronoun to an earlier sentence and a candidate component as evidence.
This resolution is the one judgment only an \ac{LLM} can make, and it is where \linkerC does its real work.
For illustration, suppose a later sentence reads ``It stores the user's settings,'' naming no component: \linkerC resolves ``It'' to an earlier sentence that named \texttt{preferences} and proposes the link to \texttt{preferences}, reporting that earlier sentence as its evidence.
The resolver is not left to find the antecedent in the catalog at large.
Each case carries, computed in code from the same writing relation, the components the sentences above it actually name, each with the sentence that names it, nearest first; the model quotes the referring expression, says which of those entries it could point to, and then names the one it does, or none.
The division is between what is a fact and what is a weighing: which components the earlier sentences name is settled exactly by the relation, and asking the model to re-derive it from the raw catalog is asking it to do lookup by attention, which it does by inventing. Which reading a referring expression carries in its sentence is in no table, so only the model can weigh it.
\approach admits a resolution only when it reports an antecedent, and checks both sentence numbers it reports against the document, so a resolution citing a sentence the document does not have cannot become a candidate.
It does not require the antecedent to name the component, so \approach states the requirement to the resolver and leaves the verdict to the judge.
The shortlist is a prior and not a constraint for the same reason: the resolver may answer outside it, and what the code checks is the answer's sentence numbers, not its membership in the list.
The surviving candidates go to the \corefValidator{}, which is shown the referring expression and the antecedent the resolver committed to.

\begin{promptL}{\linkerC: Coreference Resolution}{coref}\\
{\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
Resolve refer-back references (pronoun or\\
noun phrase) to components.\\
COMPONENTS: \{comp\_names\}\\
SENTENCES: \{Sn: text\}\\
\{per case: TARGET Sn + NAMED BEFORE THIS\\
~~CASE: Name (Sm), ... nearest first\}\\
Quote the referring expression, then the\\
entries it could point to, then the one\\
it does.\\
\{COREF\_RULE\}\\
Return JSON:\\
~~\{"resolutions": [\{case, sent, ref,\\
~~~~candidates, component, ant\_sent,\\
~~~~ant\_text\}]\}
}
\end{promptL}

\subsection{Evidence-Backed Judges}\label{sec:validators}

\acp{LLM} bring rich semantic understanding but are non-deterministic and can hallucinate.
Left unchecked, this would bring false positive links.
Two observations shape the judge design.

First, a judge should rule on a candidate from its evidence, not guess at it again.
Instead of re-classifying the link from scratch, each judge checks the candidate against the evidence bundle the linker reported.
This keeps the judge and the linker working from the same text.
Where a plain \ac{LLM}-as-a-judge sees only the candidate link, we augment the judge's input with that evidence bundle (the matched span, the reference form, the preceding sentence, and the other sentences that state the component's name, plus the antecedent sentence for a coreference link), which is what makes each judge \emph{context-augmented}.
The reference form is the one field of the bundle that is itself computed rather than quoted: it records whether the sentence states the name in proper case standing alone, in lowercase prose, inside a qualified identifier, or through a discovered alias.
Every judge must also quote the words it ruled on before it gives a verdict.
What that quote is worth depends on what else the candidate carries.
For \linkerD it is the only evidence there is, so it is checked mechanically: a verdict counts only when the quote is an exact substring of the sentence and the anchor it cites is one of the sentences the judge was offered, and a fabricated quote cannot approve a link.
For \linkerB and \linkerC the sentence already carries a name match, and there the quote does its work by being demanded rather than by being checked: it forces the judge to commit to text before deciding.

Second, the three linkers pose different questions, so their judges do different work.
A named mention has to clear two bars: the sentence must be \emph{relevant}, an architectural action that belongs to the component, and it must be \emph{unique}, pointing to that component rather than another or to a generic use of the word.
A coreference link asks one question instead, because the resolver has already committed to both halves of the claim (the referring expression and the sentence it read as the antecedent), leaving the judge only to rule on whether that commitment holds.
\approach puts both bars for a named mention in one call, stated in the judge's rubric rather than in a focus line.

Every judge therefore rules once, and what separates them is the evidence each is handed and how hard its quote is checked.
The \entValidator{} sees the matched span, the reference form and the sentences that name the component elsewhere, and weighs relevance and uniqueness together against them.
The \partValidator{} is shown no target at all, so its quote is the whole of its evidence and is the one that is checked mechanically.
The \corefValidator{} is shown the referring expression and the antecedent the resolver committed to, and rules on whether that commitment holds.
We call this design \emph{\evidenceBacked judging}: each judge rules on a candidate from the evidence bundle its linker reported, and it is the bundle, not a second call, that makes the verdict checkable. We treat it as a method contribution.

\begin{promptL}{Evidence-Backed Judge}{judge}\\
{\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
Validate components. \{FOCUS\}\\
COMPONENTS: \{comp\_names\}\\
\{VALIDATION\_RULE\}\\
CASES: \{cases\}\\
Return JSON:\\
~~\{"validations": [\{case,\\
~~~~claim: "quote|none", approve\}]\}
}
\end{promptL}

\noindent The \linkerB and \linkerC judges share this prompt: \texttt{\{VALIDATION\_RULE\}} carries either the named-mention rubric, which states the relevance and uniqueness bars together, or the coreference rubric, and \texttt{\{FOCUS\}} is empty for \linkerB. The \linkerD judge uses the target-blind prompt shown in \autoref{sec:partial-linker}.
The checked links are then output as final recovered links.
