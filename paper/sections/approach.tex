We propose \approach, a training-free \ac{LLM} workflow that recovers trace links between an architecture document and an architectural model.
Its input is a document $D$ with sentences $S = \{s_1, \ldots, s_n\}$ and a component set $C = \{c_1, \ldots, c_m\}$.
Its output is a link set $L \subseteq S \times C$.
Each link $(s_i, c_j) \in L$ asserts that sentence $s_i$ discusses the architectural responsibilities of component $c_j$.

Recovering these links has three challenges, and the workflow answers each with one design decision.
First, the project's vocabulary is not given in advance: a component appears under aliases the document coins as it goes, and some component names are ordinary English words that can't be simply matched. The workflow must build this knowledge before it links.
Second, links are carried by different reference forms.
A sentence may state a component's name or an alias outright;
it may carry only one word of that name, with the name itself stated elsewhere;
or it may name nothing and lean on a pronoun resolved to an earlier sentence.
One linker cannot serve all three well.
Third, an \ac{LLM} can return a link that reads as plausible but has no support in the sentence, 
so each link must be checked against its own evidence.

\textbf{\approach in a nutshell.}
\approach answers the three challenges in three steps (\autoref{fig:approach-overview}).
It first builds a runtime \emph{knowledge module}, an alias table computed once from the components and the document.
A sentence then gives a component's whole name, part of it, or only a reference to it, and \approach runs three linkers in that order: the \linkerB, the \linkerD, and the \linkerC, each enhanced by the knowledge and each seeing only what the earlier ones left unlinked.
Each proposed link is checked by an \evidenceBacked judge, and the surviving links are merged into the output.
By recovering the references other tools silently drop, \approach{} aims to leave no documented component abandoned---the abandonment that the doc-model \cmrname{} (\cmr) of \autoref{sec:metric} measures.

\subsection{Design of \approach}\label{sec:arch}

Three decisions fix the shape of the workflow.

The first decision answers the project-knowledge challenge: the workflow gathers what it needs to know about a project once, up front, rather than leaving each linker to work it out on its own.
The underlying difficulty is the vocabulary problem~\cite{furnas1987vocabulary}: one component can appear under many names, and some of those names are ordinary English, so plain lexical matching is unreliable.
\approach builds this knowledge before linking, as a separate stage, in the spirit of glossary mining for requirements~\cite{arora2017automated,gemkow2018automatic} and software-side synonym networks~\cite{howard2013automatically,falleri2010automatic}.

The second decision answers the reference-form challenge: how to split the linking work.
Let $N(c)$ be the \emph{names} of a component $c$: its model name $\mathrm{name}(c)$ and the project-specific aliases $A(c)$ that the knowledge module discovers---its abbreviations and synonyms.

A sentence may write a name in more ways than one, and \approach needs a single, stated
notion of when it has.
We write $s \models_{f} n$ when sentence $s$ \emph{writes} name $n$ at \emph{form} $f$, over two independent dimensions.
\emph{Fidelity} says how exactly the characters must reproduce the name: \emph{as spelled} (character for character, at word boundaries), \emph{any case} (ignoring capitalization), or \emph{any spelling} (the name's word sequence, ignoring case, separators and compound joining, so that \texttt{Image Provider}, \texttt{image-provider} and \texttt{ImageProvider} are one form).
\emph{Extent} says how much of the name must be present: the whole name, or \emph{any word} of it under an English inflectional ending.
This relation reads only the model catalog and the alias table, together with one closed list of English suffixes; it contains no project vocabulary of any kind.

\autoref{tab:forms} is why the linkers are ordered as they are.
Each row reports how many sentence--component pairs the form reaches across the five benchmark documents and how many of them are true links.
Precision falls monotonically as the form loosens and reach rises, and \approach answers that with the judge behind it rather than with a further rule: the tightest forms feed a judge that approves by default, the loosest feeds a judge that is shown no target, and sentences no form reaches at all are left to a judge that rejects when uncertain.
The two looser forms carry one shared restriction, that a surface owned by more than one component is evidence for neither.
Nothing in the relation admits a link on its own---every form produces a \emph{candidate} for an \ac{LLM} judge.

The forms are almost, but not quite, nested, and the exceptions are informative rather than incidental: compound splitting reaches \texttt{Image Provider} for a component named \texttt{ImageProvider}, which no case-folding rule reaches, and misses \texttt{redis pubsub} for one named \texttt{Redis PubSub}, which case folding does reach.
Compound splitting is therefore a different normalization, not a looser one, and \approach takes the union of the forms a linker scans.

A sentence $s$ then writes a full name in $N(c)$, part of one, or none of one.
These ask different questions---whether a sentence writes a name, whether it carries one word of a name written elsewhere, or whether it writes no name and must be resolved through the earlier sentence it refers back to---so they need different evidence and different judges.
\approach therefore uses three linkers and tries them in that order (\linkerB, then \linkerD, then \linkerC), rather than the one large \ac{LLM} call that previous works used.
Each linker runs once and sees only what the earlier ones left unlinked, so the strongest available name evidence decides a link and the weaker linkers are left the sentences it cannot reach.
This is not only bookkeeping. Because a linker never re-proposes a pair the pipeline already holds, the later judges rule on the sentences still in question instead of on a majority of settled ones: over five runs on all five projects, withholding the settled pairs from the \linkerC{} judge removes $6.8$ false positives ($p=0.01$) at $+0.8$ true positives ($p=0.05$).
The \linkerC retains the \emph{structural antecedent constraint}: a resolution is accepted only when the antecedent sentence itself writes a name in $N(c)$.
That is the same relation at the same form the \linkerB admits on and the \linkerD defers on---one condition read by three stages, not three conditions.

The third decision answers the reliability challenge: how to handle the non-determinism of the \ac{LLM}.
An \ac{LLM} linker can return a wrong link that still reads as plausible, with nothing in the sentence to support it.
\approach therefore makes each linker report the text evidence it used in a structured \emph{evidence bundle} and 
has each judge check the candidate link against that. 
Every judge must quote the sentence words it rules on before it rules, and where the sentence carries no name for the judge to fall back on, that quote is checked mechanically against the sentence (\autoref{sec:validators}).

\subsection{Project Knowledge Discovery}\label{sec:knowledge}
Stage~1 is a \emph{document-understanding module} that analyzes the document against the component set to find the aliases the project has coined for its components.

An earlier design paired it with a \emph{model-understanding module} that asked an \ac{LLM} which component names double as ordinary English---in JabRef (\autoref{fig:example}) the component \texttt{preferences} shares its name with the everyday word---and passed the answer to the judges as an advisory flag.
The flag reached $78$ of $188$ candidates, so it was not idle, but removing the module, its call and its flag changes neither score ($\mathrm{TP}$ $-0.2$, $p=1.00$; $\mathrm{FP}$ $+0.8$, $p=0.40$ over five runs on all five projects).
\approach therefore leaves ordinary-English ambiguity to the judges, which see the sentence itself, and keeps one table instead of two.

\subsubsection{Alias Discovery}\label{sec:doc-understanding}
An alias lets a linker match a document phrase to a component even when the phrase is not the component's exact name.
\approach discovers these aliases from the document at runtime, and they come in two kinds.
The first is an explicit renaming, where the document itself ties a short form to a name: an abbreviation in a parenthetical definition, or a synonym in a clear restatement.
The second is a shorthand, one word of a multi-word name used on its own after the document has introduced the name.
Shorthands are common, but many are also generic English whose meaning depends on the surrounding text, so this is the hard kind.
An \ac{LLM} judge confirms each proposed mapping before it enters the alias table, rejecting a phrase that is generic vocabulary, names the whole system, names a different entity, or names a group of elements rather than one.
Every confirmed alias is then a name of its component wherever it appears, on the same footing as the model name.
An earlier design instead graded each alias by how distinctive it was and withheld the ordinary-English ones from \linkerB{}; grading them cost $3.0$ true positives ($p=0.01$) for no measurable gain in precision ($+1.0$ false positives, $p=0.59$), so the grade was dropped and the judges are left to decide in context.

\subsection{\linkerB}\label{sec:entity-linker}

% Without the knowledge module, a named-mention linker would handle the all named mention forms from scratch.
% The alias table removes this need: once it exists, every named form is just a sentence in $s$ that matches the exact name or one of the recorded aliases, so a single extraction step covers them all.
\linkerB reads each sentence together with the alias table and asks the \ac{LLM} to mark the sentences that describe a component, 
returning a candidate link $(s,c)$.
Because the extractor is deliberately lenient, \approach then enforces the linker's own contract with the writing relation: a candidate is kept only when its sentence writes some name in $N(c)$ at \emph{any case}.
The same relation also runs on its own, at the two forms an \ac{LLM} extractor is most likely to miss, so that the extractor is a proposer and not a single point of failure.
Scanning at \emph{any spelling} recovers the orthographic variants of a name; scanning at \emph{as spelled} places a recall floor under the extractor.
The floor is drawn at the tightest form for the reason \autoref{tab:forms} gives: a component named \texttt{Common} or \texttt{Client} matches ordinary English on every page, and capitalization is what separates the proper noun from the common one, so the same scan run case-insensitively would trade a precision of $0.96$ for one of $0.77$.
Neither scan admits anything; both hand their pairs to the same judge as the extractor's own.
Each candidate is tagged with the matched sentence and the reference form, forming the candidate's evidence bundle.
%DONE 2026-08-10 (code audit): dropped "and a short reason" -- the bundle's extraction_rationale field is
%   the same constant string for every candidate, so it carries no per-candidate information to report.
Candidates and their evidence bundles will be the input for the link judge.

For example, in \autoref{fig:example} Sentence~11 names the \texttt{preferences} component, so \linkerB returns the candidate $(s_{11}, \texttt{preferences})$ with an evidence bundle recording the matched name ``preferences'' and the reference form as the reason.
Sentence~7 uses the same word ``preferences'' while its topic is \texttt{gui}.
Although we prompt the \ac{LLM} to propose a link only when the topic is the matched component, the \ac{LLM} may not follow the rule or may be unable to understand architectural roles.
If \linkerB also proposes the false link $(s_{7}, \texttt{preferences})$, it will be the judge's responsibility to reject it.

\subsection{\linkerD}\label{sec:partial-linker}

\linkerD reaches the sentences that carry only one word of a component's name.
It scans the same writing relation at its loosest form, \emph{any word}: it proposes a candidate $(s,c)$ when $s$ writes a name in $N(c)$ at that form and no other component of the model does.
It considers only sentences that write no whole name in $N(c)$, because those sentences already belong to \linkerB.
For example, a document that names an \texttt{HTML5 Client} may later write only ``each client'': \linkerD proposes that link and records the matched word as its evidence.
One word is weak evidence on its own, since an ordinary English word may carry no architectural reference at all, so \linkerD judges in two steps.
It first asks, \emph{without showing the component}, whether the expression denotes a software participant or something merely associated with software; only the surviving expressions are then compared with the component, grounded in a sentence that states one of its names.
Withholding the component in the first step is what makes the step informative: shown the target, the \ac{LLM} tends to confirm identity rather than test it.

\begin{promptL}{\linkerD: Denotation Without the Target}{denote}\\
{\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
Classify what each expression itself denotes\\
in its local context: participant for a\\
software participant, or associated for\\
something merely associated with software.\\
SENTENCES: \{Sn: text\}\\
CASES: \{case, source, expression\}\\
Claim must be an exact substring of the\\
source sentence.\\
Return JSON:\\
~~\{"judgments": [\{case, denotation,\\
~~~~claim\}]\}
}
\end{promptL}

\subsection{\linkerC}\label{sec:coref-linker}

\linkerC recovers the links in sentences that write no name at all, at any form of the relation.
For each sentence with a pronoun like 'it',  an \ac{LLM} step resolves the pronoun to an earlier sentence and a candidate component as evidence.
This resolution is the one judgment only an \ac{LLM} can make, and it is where \linkerC does its real work.
For illustration, suppose a later sentence reads ``It stores the user's settings,'' naming no component: \linkerC resolves ``It'' to an earlier sentence that named \texttt{preferences} and proposes the link to \texttt{preferences}, reporting that earlier sentence as its evidence.

\subsection{Evidence-Backed Judges}\label{sec:validators}

\acp{LLM} bring rich semantic understanding but are non-deterministic and can hallucinate.
Left unchecked, this would bring false positive links.
Two observations shape the judge design.

First, a judge should rule on a candidate from its evidence, not guess at it again.
Instead of re-classifying the link from scratch, each judge checks the candidate against the evidence bundle the linker reported.
This keeps the judge and the linker working from the same text.
Where a plain \ac{LLM}-as-a-judge sees only the candidate link, we augment the judge's input with that evidence bundle---the matched span, the reference form, the preceding sentence, and the other sentences that state the component's name, plus the antecedent sentence for a coreference link---which is what makes each judge \emph{context-augmented}.
The reference form is the one field of the bundle that is itself computed rather than quoted: it records whether the sentence states the name in proper case standing alone, in lowercase prose, inside a qualified identifier, or through a discovered alias.
Withholding it costs $6.6$ true positives ($p=0.01$), so the judge is using it.
Its distinctions are finer than they look necessary: the judge approves proper-case standalone mentions and lowercase ones at $96.9\%$ and $100.0\%$, so the grade appears to carry nothing.
Merging those two values anyway costs $0.9$ macro F1 ($p=0.05$ over six paired runs), concentrated on the shortest document, where a shift of one and a half false positives moves F1 by four points.
Equal approval rates per value are therefore not evidence that a distinction is inert.
Every judge must also quote the words it ruled on before it gives a verdict.
What that quote is worth depends on what else the candidate carries.
For \linkerD it is the only evidence there is, so it is checked mechanically: a verdict counts only when the quote is an exact substring of the sentence and the anchor it cites is one of the sentences the judge was offered, and a fabricated quote cannot approve a link.
For \linkerB and \linkerC the sentence already carries a name match, and there the quote does its work by being demanded rather than by being checked---it forces the judge to commit to text before deciding.
Both halves of that claim are measured.
Dropping the quote from those two prompts costs $35.2$ true positives ($p=0.01$), so the demand is not decoration; adding the mechanical check to them voids nothing at all ($0$ verdicts in $25$ project-runs), so the check would be.

%   held only for \linkerD, whose judge verifies claim-substring + anchor membership in code
%   (_classify_denotations / _review_identity_batch). The \linkerB p1/p2 and \linkerC judges request a
%   claim quote and discard it (_run_validation_pass reads only "approve"). Scope now stated explicitly.

Of the partial-name judge's two steps, only the first is load-bearing.
The target-blind step is worth $12$ false positives.
The grounded review that follows it, which shows the model the target together with the sentences naming it and asks whether the two denote the same participant, trades recall for precision at a bad rate: it rejects $8.0$ candidates per run of which $5.5$ are gold.
Dropping it leaves F1 unchanged ($+0.2$, $p=0.53$) and improves F2 by $1.3$ ($p=0.01$) over six paired runs, and it helps only one of the five documents, the one that proposes the most partial names, while costing recall on the one where partial names carry the most links.

Second, the named-mention and coreference linkers pose different questions, so their judges do different work.
A named mention has to clear two bars: the sentence must be \emph{relevant}, an architectural action that belongs to the component, and it must be \emph{unique}, pointing to that component rather than another or to a generic use of the word.
A coreference link is simpler, because the alias lookup in \linkerC has already dropped every candidate whose earlier sentence does not name the component, so one question remains: whether the resolution fits the content of that earlier sentence.
Giving \linkerC{} a second pass as well confirms this rather than improving on it: it moves neither score ($\mathrm{TP}$ $-0.6$, $p=0.40$; $\mathrm{FP}$ $-0.8$, $p=0.17$).
Resolution and judging read the same batch size, so the workflow states two batch sizes rather than three; giving resolution a smaller window of its own costs a quarter of the pipeline's calls and buys nothing ($\mathrm{F1}$ $-0.2$, $p=0.52$; $\mathrm{F2}$ $-0.0$, $p=0.91$ over six paired runs).
The two bars for a named mention are asked in two separate calls, and that separation---rather than the number of calls---is what earns the precision.
Asking both in one call costs $0.9$ macro F1 and $3.5$ false positives (both $p=0.01$ over six paired runs); asking that merged call twice and requiring it to agree with itself recovers about a third of the loss.
The reason is visible in the verdicts: two prompts that pose different questions disagree on $2.7\%$ of candidates, of which four in five are false positives the conjunction removes, whereas two samples of one merged prompt disagree on only $0.6\%$ while rejecting exactly the same candidates unanimously.
Independence between verdicts comes from asking a different question, not from sampling the same one again.

The \entValidator{} therefore runs two passes, a relevance pass $p_1$ and a uniqueness pass $p_2$, and accepts a candidate only when both hold ($p_1 \land p_2$), since a trace link needs both.
The \corefValidator{} runs a single pass on its one remaining question, since the alias lookup has already done what a second pass would repeat.
We call this design \emph{\evidenceBacked judging}: each judge rules on a candidate from the evidence bundle the linker reported, with the number of passes matched to how hard the decision is. We treat it as a method contribution.

\begin{promptL}{Evidence-Backed Judge}{judge}\\
{\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
Validate components. \{FOCUS\}\\
COMPONENTS: \{comp\_names\}\\
\{VALIDATION\_RULE\}\\
CASES: \{cases\}\\
Return JSON:\\
~~\{"validations": [\{case,\\
~~~~claim: "quote|none", approve\}]\}
}
\end{promptL}

\noindent All judges share this prompt; \texttt{\{FOCUS\}} and \texttt{\{VALIDATION\_RULE\}} are instantiated three times: the \linkerB relevance pass $p_1$ and uniqueness pass $p_2$, and the \linkerC single coreference-fit pass.
The checked links are then output as final recovered links.
