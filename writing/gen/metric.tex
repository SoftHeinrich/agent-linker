\section{Architecture-Driven Evaluation of Trace-Link Recovery}
\label{sec:metric}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:metric:bias}

\subsubsection*{Background}
The standard evaluation for doc-to-code trace-link recovery uses the benchmark released with TransArc~\cite{transarc,replication}.
Five Java systems come with a doc, an architecture model, and a hand-curated gold standard.
Each gold entry is a decision by a human annotator: a sentence in the doc is linked to either a single code file or, more often, to a directory.
At evaluation time, each directory entry is \emph{enrolled}: it is expanded to all individual code files inside that directory.
After enrollment, precision, recall, and \fone are computed over (sentence, file) pairs.

\autoref{tab:enrollment} shows how strongly enrollment changes the data on which \fone is computed.
The 525 raw human decisions become 18,660 file-level data points, with project-specific factors ranging from 1.0$\times$ to 217.6$\times$.

\subsubsection*{The benchmark is structurally unequal}
The standard \fone treats each (sentence, file) pair as one equally weighted data point.
The benchmark's structure breaks this assumption in three ways at once.

\emph{Enrollment inflation.}
One human decision becomes between 1 and 972 file-level data points.
On JabRef, 38 decisions become 8,268 points (217.6$\times$), so a single directory decision can swing \fone by up to four percentage points.
The metric appears to be computed on thousands of data points, but it really reflects a few dozen human decisions.

\emph{Block correlation.}
Files enrolled from the same directory share the same outcome: a system either identifies that component or it does not.
We measured block homogeneity (whether all files in a block are correct or all wrong) and found it ranges from 96\% to 100\% across the five projects.
File-level pairs are therefore not independent data points; they are redundant copies of one decision.

\emph{Gold concentration.}
In every project, a small number of components dominates the gold standard.
The top three components hold between 38\% and 99\% of all file-level gold links, and the single top component accounts for 45\%–48\% of the gold in four out of five projects.
On JabRef, three components alone produce 98.6\% of the file-level gold.

\subsubsection*{What this means for the metric}
Under heavy structural inequality, \fone is dominated by the few largest items.
Getting JabRef's three big components right yields \fone\,$>$\,0.95 regardless of how the system performs on the remaining components.
Two consequences follow.

First, the same \fone number means different things on different projects.
A file-level \fone of 0.94 on JabRef summarises performance on essentially three components; the same \fone on MediaStore summarises performance on roughly 50 mostly file-level decisions.
Cross-project comparison under one \fone is therefore not meaningful, even though the literature routinely averages it.

Second, a system that improves on the harder, smaller decisions is rewarded only weakly.
Improvements that change one large-component outcome are amplified into hundreds of file-level true positives, while improvements that change many small-component outcomes count for only a few points each.
The standard ruler is most sensitive to the part of the benchmark with the least information, and least sensitive to the part where systems differ most.

\subsubsection*{Stance}
This paper makes two co-equal contributions: AALinker (\autoref{sec:approach}) and the metric suite defined below.
We motivate the metric suite from the structure of the benchmark, not from AALinker's results.
Each metric is chosen to correct one of the three biases above, or to expose a property of trace-link quality that \fone is structurally unable to surface (sentence coverage, per-sentence noise, per-component fairness, cross-project comparability).
We then evaluate AALinker and all baselines under both the standard ruler and the new rulers, so future work can be compared honestly under either.

The metric story is not "we win bigger under the new metrics".
As we will show in \autoref{sec:exp:rq2}, AALinker leads under every metric we report, but the standard \fone underreports the gain: the gap over TransArc is $+12.9$ percentage points on file-level \fone and $+22.7$ percentage points on the metric matched to a single human annotator decision.
The standard ruler is the one that hides the improvement, because the same structural inequality that inflates good performance on a few large components also dilutes per-decision gains into the average.

The picture also varies by project, and we report this honestly rather than averaging it away.
Per-component \fone shows the smallest aggregate gap ($+10.3$pp) of any metric, but this average hides one Teammates regression at component level ($-9$pp) against four projects where AALinker improves by $+4$pp (JabRef) to $+35$pp (MediaStore).
We discuss this Teammates pattern in \autoref{sec:exp:rq2} and \autoref{sec:discussion}; it does not change the overall ranking, but it confirms that the new metrics resolve effects that the corpus-level \fone smooths over.

\subsection{Metric Suite}
\label{sec:metric:suite}

We propose four primary metrics.
Each one addresses a structural weakness of standard file-level \fone\ identified in \autoref{sec:metric:bias} or surfaces a property of trace-link quality that \fone\ cannot express.
Three further metrics (per-sentence \fone, a coverage--purity composite, and a skill score) are reported in the appendix; a redundancy analysis (\autoref{sec:metric:prestudy}) shows they carry the same ranking signal as the four primary metrics and do not change the conclusions.

\paragraph{File-level \fone\ (reference).}
The standard metric used in prior work: micro \fone\ over all (sentence, file) pairs.
We retain it as the comparison point with prior work, not because we endorse it, but because the literature reports it.

\paragraph{Per-component \fone\ (macro).}
For each component~$c$, compute file-level \fone\ over the (sentence, file) pairs that touch~$c$, then average across all components.
This gives every architectural component equal weight, so a system is no longer rewarded for getting a few large components right while ignoring small ones.
Corrects the gold-concentration and enrollment-inflation biases of \autoref{sec:metric:bias}.

\paragraph{Sentence coverage.}
The fraction of gold sentences for which the system produced at least one correct link.
Asks the binary question per sentence: \emph{did the system serve this sentence at all?}
Independent of how many wrong links the system added---only counts correct hits.

\paragraph{Noise rate.}
For each sentence the system predicted at least one link for, compute the wrong-link share $\text{FP}_s / (\text{TP}_s + \text{FP}_s)$, then macro-average across predicted sentences (silent sentences are excluded from the denominator).
Reports \emph{per sentence, how messy is the output?}
This is the only metric in the suite that catches over-prediction distributed across many sentences; file-level and per-component \fone\ can both hide such behaviour when the false positives are spread thinly.

\subsection{Metric Validation Pre-Study}
\label{sec:metric:prestudy}

Before applying the metric suite to real systems (\autoref{sec:exp:rq2}), we check that each primary metric resists trivial inflation on both link tasks: doc-to-code and doc-to-model.

\paragraph{Structural Top-K baselines.}
A \emph{naive Top-K baseline} predicts the same fixed set of $K$ components for every sentence in the doc, regardless of sentence content.
The $K$ components are chosen by a \emph{structural ranking} computed from architecture-model or doc features alone, without ever consulting the gold standard.
A bias-resistant metric refuses to credit such a baseline beyond chance, no matter which structural feature the ranking exploits.

The choice of ranking feature is task-specific because the human annotation process correlates with a different signal on each task.
On doc-to-code, one human directory decision expands into all files in that directory, so the structural feature most correlated with gold-link mass is \emph{component file count}---a few large components dominate the file-level gold by construction.
On doc-to-model, each human decision is one (sentence, component) link, and the feature most correlated with gold mass is \emph{in-doc name frequency}---components whose names appear in the doc attract most annotator decisions.

We use $K{=}3$ with two task-specific structural rankings: file count on doc-to-code, in-doc case-insensitive name frequency on doc-to-model.
Both rules use only structural signals (architecture-model file counts, doc text); neither uses gold.
For comparison we also run \emph{Random}, which samples (sentence, target) pairs at the gold link density and serves as a zero-skill anchor.

\paragraph{Doc-to-code: reversal under enrollment.}
The structural Top-3 baseline averages 0.30 file-level \fone\ against \emph{Random}'s 0.15---a roughly two-fold inflation that file-level \fone\ credits despite zero real link-prediction skill.
Per-component \fone\ reverses the ranking on the three projects with non-trivial enrollment expansion---JabRef (217.6$\times$), Teammates (35.5$\times$), and BigBlueButton (11.6$\times$)---where \emph{Random}'s scattered hits (mean 0.23) beat Top-3's concentrated mass (mean 0.18) under macro averaging.
Sentence coverage reverses on four of the five projects (all except MediaStore), with macro means 0.69 (Random) vs 0.46 (Top-3); the reversal extends to TeaStore (10.1$\times$ enrollment) because coverage is sensitive to broad scatter even at moderate enrollment, while per-component \fone\ needs heavier structural inequality to flip.
MediaStore (enrollment factor 1.0$\times$, no structural inequality) is the only project where neither metric reverses---exactly as expected when there is no bias to expose.
Noise rate confirms the inflation has no per-sentence basis: both baselines average $\geq 0.80$ wrong-link share per predicted sentence (macro mean 0.85 for \emph{Random}, 0.80 for Top-3), while real systems sit below 0.30 on the same metric.
The two-fold file-level \fone\ gap between Top-3 and \emph{Random} is therefore structural, not behavioural.

\paragraph{Doc-to-model: exposure of the narrative split.}
The structural Top-3 baseline averages 0.28 micro \fone\ against \emph{Random}'s 0.12---also a roughly two-fold inflation, but with a different mechanism.
Doc-to-model gold has no enrollment expansion; the inflation comes from popularity skew (the top-3 components hold 52--78\,\% of gold links per project; Gini $\approx 0.62$).
The metric suite responds differently here than on doc-to-code: per-component \fone\ narrows the inflation (0.14 vs 0.08) but does not reverse, because architecture models contain few components (6--22 per project) and macro averaging is less punishing on Top-3.
The strongest discriminators are sentence coverage and noise rate.
Across projects, 44\,\% of doc sentences carry no gold link---the \emph{narrative/referential split}, ranging from 23\,\% on JabRef to 77\,\% on Teammates---so a baseline that predicts components for every sentence accumulates noise rate 0.79.
Micro \fone\ cannot diagnose this because narrative sentences contribute no pairs to either side of the ratio.
Noise rate plays the same role here as on doc-to-code: it exposes Top-K's predict-everything strategy uniformly, independent of which structural inequality the task carries.

\paragraph{Take-away.}
The metric suite earns its place on both tasks for different reasons.
On doc-to-code it \emph{reverses} the structural Top-3 baseline on the high-enrollment projects, resisting enrollment-driven inflation.
On doc-to-model it \emph{exposes} the narrative/referential split that micro \fone\ cannot diagnose, and narrows the popularity-skew inflation that micro \fone\ amplifies.
Per-component \fone\ and sentence coverage are task-conditional discriminators---they reverse where the task's structural bias is severe---while noise rate is a task-uniform discriminator: it punishes the predict-everything strategy on both tasks.
The combination covers both bias-driven and strategy-driven triviality, independent of which task's bias dominates.
